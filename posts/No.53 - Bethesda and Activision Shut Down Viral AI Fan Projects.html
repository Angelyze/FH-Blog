Bethesda and Activision have reportedly issued DMCA takedowns against viral, AI-assisted decompilation projects of Fallout and Modern Warfare 2.

Editorial note: This article is based on press reporting and has not been confirmed by the developer or publisher.

A new wave of 'vibe-coded' fan recreations has reportedly drawn swift legal action from major publishers.

According to a report by The Gamer, major publishers are moving fast to rein in a sudden wave of viral, AI-assisted fan recreations. Modders and fan developers have dealt with DMCA notices for years, but these rapid takedowns signal a fresh shift in how studios view decompilations assembled with generative tools. While neither Bethesda nor Activision has issued an official statement, the era of 'vibe-coding' iconic video game franchises is clearly bumping into hard legal realities.

Key facts

  • Reported by The Gamer; not yet confirmed by the developer or publisher.
  • A browser-playable project titled 'Fallout: New York' was reportedly shut down after creator ChrisFirst received a cease-and-desist from Bethesda.
  • The Fallout project utilized Claude Opus 5.5 to generate quests, dialogue, VATS, and a working Pip-Boy entirely through code without original textures or audio files.
  • Activision reportedly targeted a Modern Warfare 2 decompilation by Maurice Heumann, which utilized 17 AI agents working simultaneously.
  • Unlike traditional manual decompilations, these projects relied heavily on generative AI prompts to assemble playable builds in days rather than years.

The Rise of the Vibe-Coded Wasteland

For decades, the Fallout modding scene has earned its reputation through meticulous manual labor and years of hobbyist coding. But over the past week, a trend called 'vibe-coding' picked up massive traction on social media, using generative AI models to construct playable game systems at breakneck speed. As reported by The Gamer, even established community efforts like the Zelda decompilation Ship of Harkinian have leaned on forms of genAI, but those took time to mature. These newer viral projects rely on far more aggressive automation. Instead of spending years dissecting assembly code, creators are prompting AI models to sketch out recognizable cities, mechanics, and UI overnight—growing so visible so quickly that corporate legal teams stepped in almost immediately.

What Was 'Fallout: New York'?

The biggest Fallout effort in the crosshairs was 'Fallout: New York,' put together by a developer going by ChrisFirst on Twitter. ChrisFirst posted gameplay footage showing a browser-playable recreation of Fallout powered entirely by Claude Opus 5.5. The build featured functioning quests, working dialogue trees, VATS targeting, and a coded Pip-Boy interface. What set it apart technically was its asset pipeline: it didn't rip or package original game textures or sound files. Every city building, weapon, character face, and ambient audio cue was generated strictly through code. The clip pulled in millions of views, but the attention proved costly. ChrisFirst subsequently confirmed receiving a cease-and-desist from Bethesda, promptly shutting the project down.

Activision Targets AI-Decompiled Modern Warfare 2

Bethesda was not the only publisher issuing notices this week. Developer Maurice Heumann ran into similar roadblocks with Activision after attempting to decompile 2009's Modern Warfare 2 on PC using generative tools. Heumann stated that he had 17 AI agents operating concurrently to reconstruct the shooter faster than any human team could manage manually. He claimed the build had advanced to a fully playable state where most bugs were resolved, noting he had even managed to earn an in-game tactical nuke. Still, automated workflows come with quirks; Heumann mentioned his agents occasionally went rogue and deleted his local files. Activision swiftly stepped in, having the gameplay clips removed and restricting access to the repository hosting the code.

Why Publishers Are Drawing the Line Here

Decompilation itself is well-trodden ground in PC gaming. Traditional fan projects—like rebuilding Ocarina of Time to run natively on modern hardware in ultrawide at 280 frames per second—are celebrated because they represent years of careful, manual reverse engineering. That long, quiet development window usually gives fan teams breathing room under the radar. AI vibe-coding breaks that dynamic entirely. When a single hobbyist can prompt a working facsimile of a flagship IP over a couple of days and broadcast it to millions on social media, publishers treat it as an immediate threat to their trademarks rather than a distant fan homage. With takedowns already rolling out, companies seem eager to draw a sharp boundary before vibe-coding becomes standard practice.

Why fans are talking about this: When fan projects shift from labor-of-love manual coding to rapid AI generation, publishers are going to protect their IP faster than a Cazador strike.

While we still await official confirmation or statements from Bethesda and Activision regarding these specific legal notices, the message to the community is clear. The line between a tolerated fan project and a swift DMCA seems to lie in how fast and how automated the development becomes.
