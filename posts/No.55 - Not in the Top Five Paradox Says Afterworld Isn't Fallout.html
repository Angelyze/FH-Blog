Paradox's upcoming strategy game Afterworld looks like a wasteland map painter, but the studio says it owes more to the Bronze Age collapse than Fallout.

Editorial note: This article is based on press reporting and has not been confirmed by the developer or publisher.

According to a report from Rock Paper Shotgun, the upcoming post-apocalyptic grand strategy game is looking far beyond Bethesda's retro-futurism for inspiration.

Whenever a studio announces a grand strategy game set across a shattered North America, our immediate reflex as Fallout fans is to imagine map-painting with the Brotherhood or the NCR. But according to a report from Rock Paper Shotgun covering a recent dev diary, Paradox Interactive isn't building a Vault-Tec simulator. Luke Bean, content designer and self-described 'elder lore keeper' on the upcoming title Afterworld, made it clear that while he considers Fallout cool, our favorite retro-futuristic franchise didn't even crack their top five reference points during development.

Key facts

  • Reported by Rock Paper Shotgun; not yet confirmed by the developer or publisher.
  • Reported by Rock Paper Shotgun; based on a dev diary featuring content designer Luke Bean.
  • Bean stated Fallout is 'not in the top five reference points' for Afterworld.
  • The setting treats pre-war America like classical antiquity, viewing the collapse through a Bronze Age lens.
  • Factions include Pacific Fleet remnants, Indigenous nations, and groups descended from Mormons and the Amish.
  • Map generation procedurally simulates 100 years of regional disasters before turn one.

Ancient Empires Instead of 1950s Kitsch

The report highlights a clean philosophical break between Afterworld and standard atomic fiction. In Bethesda's sandbox, the pre-war world is practically frozen in amber—jukeboxes still spin doo-wop vinyl, and suburban houses retain their mid-century coats of paint two centuries later. According to Bean's comments in the dev diary, Afterworld treats the United States less like yesterday's neighborhood and far more like classical antiquity or the fall of Rome. "The people of this world aren't Americans exactly," Bean explained. "They are people who remember the Americans. America was an ancient empire to them, sort of the way the Roman Empire was for us, and they live in the shadow of that history." Because the ruins left behind demonstrate terrifying, godlike technology, the survivors draw a chilling mythological conclusion: if the ancients possessed the power to do whatever they wanted with science, then their extinction couldn't have been an accident. They must have actively chosen annihilation.

Bronze Age Mysticism and Cyberpunk Scraps

Rather than leaning on atomic age optimism turned sour, Paradox is aiming for what Bean characterized as "Bronze Agey mystical cyberpunk ruins." Survival here isn't just about hoarding microfusion cells or stockpiling caps; players will spend significant campaign time actively shaping a post-collapse society's core identity against the shadow of the past. "A lot of the story is also them rediscovering the culture and history of the past," Bean noted, describing the process of figuring out how a fledgling tribe relates to an empire that vanished. Players will decide whether to call themselves Americans, embrace an entirely novel identity, resurrect forgotten pre-war ideologies, or draft fresh creeds from scratch. Bean likened the cultural mechanics to writing "the Bible of a people," determining which scraps of ancient thought actually serve their new society.

The Factions Left Behind

That thematic shift away from typical raiders and power-armored paladins carries over into the faction landscape. According to Rock Paper Shotgun, survivors encountering each other across the continent represent fragmented enclaves that weathered the storm in wildly different ways. Players can expect to cross paths with surviving American Indigenous nations, communities descended from historic Amish and Mormon enclaves, and remnants of the US Pacific Fleet carrying the descendants of evacuated Hawaiian civilians. The geography outside the mainland US also tells a bleak story. Canada exists on the periphery but won't serve as a primary focus for the core release, while Mexico is reportedly considered a total write-off in the setting's lore.

A Century of Disasters Before Turn One

One of the most intriguing mechanics highlighted in the report is how Afterworld sets up its board before you take your first turn. Rather than dropping everyone into a static, uniform nuclear wasteland, the game procedurally simulates a full century of pre-campaign calamity. "The Fall ended the world with a war," Bean explained. "But it wasn't just a war, and it's a little different every game." Procedural generation selects several regional disasters representing bizarre events that unfolded before and during the Fall. Those rolling catastrophes carve radically different scars across the continent from run to run, meaning regional borders, remaining infrastructure, resources, and local faction dispositions will look fundamentally different depending on what hit the area during that missing century.

Why fans are talking about this: Post-apocalyptic strategy doesn't need bottle caps and swing music to build an empire out of the rubble.

Hands-on impressions and deeper gameplay breakdowns are still coming as Paradox continues development on Afterworld following earlier showings at Gamescom. While the instinct to compare any ruined American map to Fallout is second nature around here, seeing a team lean into genuine deep-time historical collapse rather than 1950s atomic kitsch is a welcome curveball. We'll be watching to see how that procedural century of history plays out in practice.
