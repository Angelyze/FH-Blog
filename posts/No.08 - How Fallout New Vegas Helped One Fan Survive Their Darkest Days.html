A Fallout: New Vegas fan shares how wandering the Mojave wasteland helped them survive a severe mental health crisis and recover during a low point.

A deeply personal story from the r/fnv community reminds us why the Mojave means so much more than just pixels and code to some of us.

Every now and then, I run across a post in our community spaces that completely stops me in my tracks. We talk a lot about builds, lore, and mods, but sometimes a fan shares something that puts the entire franchise into perspective. Over on the r/fnv subreddit, user Wide_Preparation8071 opened up about how Fallout: New Vegas served as a literal lifeline during a severe mental health crisis.

Key facts

  • Reddit user /u/Wide_Preparation8071 shared their story of recovering from a 2018-2019 mental health crisis on the r/fnv subreddit.
  • While dealing with psychosis, a bipolar diagnosis, and heavy medication side effects, they found comfort in playing Fallout: New Vegas nonstop for a month.
  • Due to cognitive difficulties, they spent their time simply wandering and doing a 'kill everything' run rather than trying to follow complex dialogues.
  • The user has since recovered, got sober, returned to school, landed a respectable job, and got engaged, but still plays the game regularly.

Finding Refuge in the Mojave

Back in 2018 and 2019, Wide_Preparation8071 was going through what they describe as a very low point. After being admitted to a psych ward for psychosis and receiving a bipolar diagnosis, they were left struggling with severe depression and avolition—a total lack of motivation or ability to feel anything. Their university classes fell by the wayside, and heavy psychiatric medications left them sleeping up to 14 hours a day with a brain that felt completely fried. The only thing they had the energy or focus to do was boot up Fallout: New Vegas.

The Simplicity of a 'Kill Everything' Run

When your brain is recovering from trauma or severe illness, even basic cognitive tasks can feel like climbing a mountain. The user recalled that they could not really follow the game's complex dialogue trees at the time. Instead of trying to parse the political machinations of the NCR or Caesar's Legion, they opted for a simpler path: a 'kill everything' run. They spent about a month doing nothing but wandering, exploring, and blowing off steam in the virtual desert. It wasn't about completing the perfect questline; it was about having a safe, low-stakes space to occupy a mind that was actively recovering.

A Lifeline in the Dark

It is a heavy thing to admit, but the user was incredibly candid about their state of mind back then. They wrote that they were fully wishing they were dead during that month, but the game kept them going. Having a virtual world to escape to gave them just enough of a distraction to survive the worst of their depressive episode. It is a sentiment that might sound extreme to outsiders, but anyone who has used a favorite game to anchor themselves during a personal storm will understand exactly what they mean.

The Road Back to Good Springs

Fortunately, this story has an incredibly bright ending. With time, a transition to a better medication regimen, and getting sober, Wide_Preparation8071 was able to return to school. Today, they have a respectable career, a fiancé they have been with for four years, and a much healthier outlook on life. They still return to New Vegas all the time, though under much happier circumstances. Looking back, they aren't even sure they would still be here today if they hadn't had that digital wasteland to keep their brain occupied while it healed.

Why fans are talking about this: Sometimes the best thing a video game can offer isn't a power fantasy, but a quiet, familiar place to rest when the real world gets too loud to handle.

It is incredibly brave of Wide_Preparation8071 to share such a vulnerable chapter of their life with the community. Stories like this are a stark reminder of the quiet, personal impact these games have on us long after the credits roll. It is why we keep playing, and why this community feels like home.
