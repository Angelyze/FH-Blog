Kotaku reports House House used under 0.1% AI code in Big Walk, triggering IGF award debates and player discussions around secret developer LLM usage.

Editorial note: This article is based on press reporting and has not been confirmed by the developer or publisher.

A tiny fraction of code sparked a massive industry debate over award eligibility and the 'hand-crafted' promise of indie games.

When we play hand-crafted indie darlings like Big Walk, we usually take it for granted that human hands wrote every single line of logic. It’s part of the charm—knowing a small team poured their literal lives into the project. But according to a detailed report from Kotaku, that assumption was recently put to the test. House House, the team behind the game, became the center of a fierce industry debate after it was revealed that undisclosed AI code was used during development. This isn't just a technicality; it’s a conversation that has split the Independent Games Festival (IGF) jury right down the middle, and for those of us who value transparency in the games we buy, it’s a bit of a gut punch.

Key facts

  • Reported by Kotaku; not yet confirmed by the developer or publisher.
  • Reported by Kotaku; House House confirmed well under 0.1% of Big Walk's code involved LLM or chatbot usage.
  • The revelation emerged from private 2027 Independent Games Festival (IGF) jury discussions regarding eligibility for the Seumas McNally Grand Prize.
  • House House did not disclose the usage on Steam because the platform currently only mandates disclosure for generative art, audio, and specific assets, not backend coding helpers.
  • IGF juror Austin Walker shared that judges engaged in 'intense, difficult, and generous' conversations over the ethics of AI in the indie space.
  • No generative AI was used for any creative assets like art or audio, according to the developer.

How the IGF Debate Surfaced

The news didn't break because of a public patch note or a sudden change to the Steam store page. Instead, it leaked out from the high-pressure environment of award season. According to Kotaku, Big Walk was a heavy hitter for the 2027 Independent Games Festival until its use of AI tools became a point of contention. Juror Austin Walker took to Bluesky to explain that the judges have spent the last month in 'intense, difficult' conversations about what generative AI means for the future of the industry. Some jurors felt so strongly about the 'stolen labor' aspect of AI that they issued a signed statement calling for an explicit disqualification line. They argued that even a fraction of a percent of AI code violates the spirit of an indie festival meant to celebrate human creativity.

House House's Defense and the Steam Loophole

House House hasn't stayed silent, clarifying that the AI usage was incredibly minimal—well under 0.1% of the total codebase. They also pointed out a technicality that many players might find surprising: Steam’s developer submission process doesn't actually require you to disclose backend coding LLMs. If you use a chatbot to help debug a script or optimize a small logic loop, Valve doesn't make you check a box. Because of that wording, Big Walk launched to universal acclaim without anyone knowing an automated tool had touched the code. For the developers, it was likely just a modern productivity tool, but for a community that prizes the 'artisanal' nature of indie games, that lack of upfront honesty is where the friction starts.

Divided Opinions: 'Public Flogging' vs. 'Stolen Labor'

The internal debate at Kotaku really mirrors what I’m seeing in our own Discord. Senior writer Rebekah Valentine, who championed the game, expressed a real sense of disappointment, noting that fans often assume studios with a history of hand-crafted detail would avoid AI entirely. On the other hand, John Walker argued that 'demolishing a great studio' over a tiny fraction of helper code feels like a public flogging that achieves very little. Meanwhile, Carolyn Petit, who was actually playing the game when the news broke, stood firm on the idea that the goal should be zero AI usage because it represents stolen labor regardless of the scale. It’s a tough spot—do we punish a stellar game for using a tool that is silently becoming industry standard, or do we hold the line to protect human creators?

Why Transparency Matters to the Community

At the end of the day, most of us just want to know what we’re buying. As Ash Parrish pointed out in the report, if House House had just been upfront about the 0.1% usage from the start, most people probably wouldn't have blinked. It’s the fact that it came out during an award dispute that makes it feel like a 'trust problem' rather than a technical one. When a game is marketed on its soul and its unique, human-driven design, any hint of automation feels like a breach of that contract. Whether you think AI is a useful tool or a plague on the arts, we can probably all agree that finding out about it through a leaked jury debate is the worst possible way to hear the news.

Why fans are talking about this: Even a tiny amount of AI code can trigger a massive backlash if it isn't disclosed upfront, especially in an indie scene that prides itself on human-centric development.

This story is still developing as the IGF hasn't officially settled on how they will handle award eligibility for games using LLM assistance. For now, Big Walk remains a fantastic game, but one that now carries a bit of a shadow. As more studios start using these tools to speed up the boring parts of coding, we’re going to need much clearer rules from platforms like Steam and festivals like the IGF to keep everyone on the same page.

Honestly, does it change how you feel about Big Walk knowing that 0.1% of the code came from an LLM, or is this just a case of the industry overreacting to a new tool?
