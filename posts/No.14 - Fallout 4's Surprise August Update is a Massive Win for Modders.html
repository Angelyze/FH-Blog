Bethesda just dropped a surprise update for Fallout 4, lifting the BA2 archive limit to 1024 and bringing massive new upgrades to console modding now.

The August 18th patch quietly solves some of the community's biggest headaches, from file size limits to PlayStation modding restrictions.

I'll be honest: I didn't have 'Bethesda drops a major engine-level modding fix' on my bingo card for this week. About 14 hours ago, a surprise update for Fallout 4 hit Steam and consoles, and while the patch notes look short, the implications for the modding scene are huge. We aren't just talking about a few stability tweaks; Bethesda has finally addressed the BA2 archive limit, a technical ceiling that has been a thorn in the side of heavy modders for years. If you're the type of player who treats the 255-plugin limit as a suggestion rather than a rule, this update was made for you.

Key facts

  • Confirmed via official Bethesda communications; coverage from Steam Community — New Vegas.
  • The BA2 archive limit has been increased from 256 to 1024, significantly expanding mod list capacity.
  • Xbox creators can now upload Creations up to 2 GB in size, doubling the previous limit.
  • PlayStation now supports archived files and new formats including .nif, .dds, .bink, and .fuz.
  • Fixed a major crash occurring when using the Steam Deck's virtual keyboard.
  • Resolved a top-tier streaming audio error that caused frequent game crashes.

Breaking the 1024 Archive Barrier

For PC players running heavy mod lists, the old 256 BA2 archive limit was a constant, invisible wall. Once you hit that cap, the game simply stopped loading assets, leading to the dreaded 'brown face' bug or missing textures. To get around it, we had to manually unpack archives into loose files—which slows down load times—or merge mods into giant, unwieldy chunks. By raising this limit to 1024, Bethesda has effectively removed one of the biggest technical bottlenecks for 'mega-modded' setups. You can now keep your load order organized and archived without worrying about the engine throwing a tantrum because you wanted a few extra high-res texture packs.

A Massive Boost for Console Creations

Console modders are getting the most tangible upgrades in this patch. On Xbox, the upload limit for Creations has been bumped to 2 GB, allowing for much larger, more detailed projects to live on the platform. But the real surprise is for the PlayStation crowd. Modding on Sony's hardware has always felt like playing on 'hard mode' due to strict asset restrictions. This update adds support for archives on PlayStation and introduces a range of new file types like .nif (geometry), .dds (textures), and .fuz (voice files). While it doesn't mean we're suddenly getting custom scripts or external assets that Sony still blocks, it gives authors much better tools to optimize what they *can* release.

Squashing the Deck and Audio Crashes

On the gameplay side, the update targets a few specific crashes that have been driving the community crazy. If you’ve been playing on Steam Deck, you’ve likely experienced the immediate crash that happens when the virtual keyboard pops up—that's finally been addressed. They also fixed a 'top crash' caused by streaming audio errors, which should make for a much more stable experience during heavy combat or in dense urban areas like downtown Boston. For those on the Microsoft Store version, you'll no longer see the game instantly die if you try to enter the Creations menu after your internet connection dips and reconnects.

Creation Kit Tweaks and Quality of Life

For the folks actually building the mods we love, the Creation Kit received some vital under-the-hood fixes. One of the most annoying bugs—where terrain edits to materials or heightmaps wouldn't generate grass properly—is now fixed. They also repaired the LipGen tool, meaning running it from the command line will actually produce .lip files again for custom dialogue. My favorite 'small' change is the updated 'Search and Replace' tool. It now matches the Starfield version of the editor, adding quick buttons to select forms in the object window or open the form editor directly. It’s a workflow improvement that makes the aging toolset feel just a little bit more modern.

UI Fixes and Error Codes

The update also brings some much-needed polish to the in-game menus. If you've ever searched for a mod, clicked on it, and then got frustrated when 'back' took you to the start of the list instead of the search results—that’s been fixed. Even better, Bethesda.net error messages now include specific error codes. This might sound boring, but it’s a godsend for troubleshooting; instead of telling support 'it doesn't work,' you can give them a code that actually points to the problem. They've also updated the game credits, likely acknowledging the teams that have been working on these recent 'next-gen' maintenance passes.

Why fans are talking about this: By expanding archive limits and fixing engine-level crashes, Bethesda is finally giving the modding community the breathing room it needs for the next decade of content.

It’s rare to see a developer return to a nearly decade-old game to fix technical limits like the BA2 archive cap, but it shows that the success of the TV show has kept Fallout 4 high on the priority list. I'll be updating our community guides on the Fallout Hub companion app to reflect these new limits and testing how the 1024 cap handles some of the heavier 'Wabbajack' lists. It's a good day to be a modder, and an even better day to be a player who just wants a stable game.
